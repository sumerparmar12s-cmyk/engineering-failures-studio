# The midnight ride: three riders, one legend

## Narration (final)

Just imagine: three men ride through the night to warn America that an army is coming. Only one of them becomes a legend. And the other two? You've probably never heard of them.

April 18th, 1775. Boston is packed with British soldiers, and the locals are... not thrilled.

The British plan a secret mission: march to Concord and seize the rebels' weapons.

[bm_george] Gentlemen, this is top secret.

Everyone in Boston knew by dinner.

The rebels set up a signal in the Old North Church. One lantern if by land, two if by sea. Two lanterns. The British are coming by boat.

Paul Revere, a silversmith, jumps on a horse and gallops into the night. And no, he never yelled "The British are coming." Everyone there was British.

[am_adam] The Regulars are coming out!

Meanwhile, rider number two, William Dawes, takes the long way around. Same mission. Same night.

They reach Lexington, warn Samuel Adams and John Hancock, and race toward Concord. Then... a British patrol.

[bm_lewis] Halt!

Revere gets caught. Dawes escapes... and falls off his horse.

So rider number three, Samuel Prescott, jumps a stone wall and makes it to Concord.

At dawn, shots ring out at Lexington. The American Revolution has begun.

Eighty-five years later, a poet wrote about that night... and left the other two out.

So Revere became a legend. And Dawes? A trivia question.

## Claim check
- April 18-19, 1775; British plan to seize colonial military stores at Concord; Old North Church lantern signal (two = by water across the Charles); Revere, a Boston silversmith, rowed to Charlestown and rode via Medford/Menotomy to Lexington; William Dawes took the land route over Boston Neck.
- Revere's words reported as "The Regulars are coming out" (colonists still considered themselves British).
- Both warned Samuel Adams and John Hancock at Lexington; a British patrol stopped them on the road to Concord; Revere was detained; Dawes escaped and was thrown from / fell off his horse; Samuel Prescott jumped a wall and reached Concord.
- Shots at Lexington at dawn April 19, 1775. Longfellow's "Paul Revere's Ride" (written 1860, published 1861), omitting Dawes and Prescott.
