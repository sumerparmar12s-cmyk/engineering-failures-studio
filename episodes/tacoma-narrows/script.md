# Galloping Gertie: The Bridge That Tore Itself Apart (≈2 min)

## Narration (final)

On the morning of November seventh, nineteen forty, a bridge in Washington State began to twist like a ribbon in the wind. Within an hour, it was gone.

The Tacoma Narrows Bridge had opened just four months earlier. Its main span stretched twenty-eight hundred feet across the Narrows of Puget Sound, the third-longest suspension span in the world. And it was astonishingly slender: a roadway just thirty-nine feet wide, stiffened by steel girders only eight feet deep.

That elegance came at a price. Even in light winds, the deck rose and fell in long, rolling waves. Drivers watched the cars ahead disappear into the dips. Locals gave the bridge a nickname: Galloping Gertie.

Then came the storm.

That November morning, a steady wind of about forty-two miles an hour swept through the Narrows. Shortly after ten, the rhythm changed. The bridge stopped galloping, and began to twist. One sidewalk climbed while the other plunged, tilting the roadway by nearly thirty degrees.

Newspaper editor Leonard Coatsworth was on the bridge when it began. He crawled to safety on his hands and knees, forced to leave his car behind, with his daughter's dog, Tubby, still inside.

Each twist fed the next. The wind was no longer just pushing the bridge. The bridge's own motion was shaping the wind, pumping energy into every cycle. Engineers would later call it aeroelastic flutter.

Just after eleven, the steel gave way. The center span tore loose and plunged into the water below. No person died that day. Tubby was the only life lost.

The investigation changed bridge design forever. Today, every major span is tested in a wind tunnel before it is built. And the wreck of Galloping Gertie still rests on the floor of Puget Sound: a lesson written in steel.
