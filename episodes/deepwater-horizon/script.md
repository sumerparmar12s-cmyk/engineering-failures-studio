# The Last 16 Hours of Deepwater Horizon (≈10.5 min)

## Narration (final)

In a few hours, this rig will be a fireball. Eleven men on board will never go home.

But right now, the people in charge are celebrating. Seven years without a single serious injury. One of the best performing rigs in the entire Gulf of Mexico.

So how does the safest rig in the Gulf explode on the very same night it is being praised for safety?

The answer was sitting on a pressure gauge, hours before the explosion. The crew looked right at it. They even argued about it. And then, they explained it away.

This is the story of the last sixteen hours of the Deepwater Horizon. Let's start the clock.

[PAUSE 1.6 clock]

Tuesday, April twentieth, 2010. 5:45 in the morning.

Fifty miles off the coast of Louisiana, a floating giant sits in the dark. The Deepwater Horizon. A drilling rig the size of a city block, with a derrick more than twenty stories tall. One hundred and twenty-six people live and work on board.

Below it, almost a mile of water. And below the seabed, a well called Macondo, drilled more than three miles into the earth, into a pocket of oil and gas under enormous pressure.

The job is almost done. The well just needs to be sealed and left behind, so another rig can come back later to produce the oil.

At the bottom of the well, a crew has just pumped cement, to plug the hole, like a cork in a bottle. A cementing engineer sends an email to Houston. Quote: "We have completed the job, and it went well."

Remember that sentence. We'll come back to it.

Eleven a.m.

A team of specialists is on board with tools that can actually check that cement, and look for weak spots. But the cement job looked fine on paper. So the decision is made: send the team home on the eleven o'clock helicopter. It saves time, and a hundred and twenty-eight thousand dollars.

[PAUSE 1.4 helicopter]

The one test that could have looked inside the well just flew away.

Afternoon. Another helicopter lands. Four executives from BP, the oil company, and Transocean, the company that owns the rig, step onto the deck for what's called a management visibility tour. This rig is a star. And on that tour, the managers will talk about safety. About preventing slips, falls and dropped tools.

Keep that in mind too. Because the danger that night wasn't a dropped tool.

Five p.m. The most important test of the day begins. It's called a negative pressure test.

Here's a simple way to think about it. Imagine you seal a bottle, and then you pull the pressure inside it down. If the seal is good, the pressure stays at zero. If the pressure starts creeping back up, something is leaking in.

That's exactly what the crew does to the well. They bleed the pressure off. And then they watch.

The pressure climbs back up.

They bleed it again. It climbs again. They bleed it a third time. It keeps coming back.

The drill shack fills up with people. The shift is changing. The visiting executives even walk through on their tour. One of them notices a discussion going on about some pressure, and moves on.

Now here's where it gets dangerous.

The night crew runs the test again, this time on a different line, called the kill line. And on that line, the pressure stays at zero. But on the drill pipe, it reads fourteen hundred psi. Two readings that should match, and don't.

According to BP witnesses, a respected senior toolpusher said he had seen this before. He called it the "bladder effect." A harmless quirk.

Investigators would later conclude there is no such thing as a "bladder effect" that could explain those readings. The only explanation was that the well was flowing. But at that moment, it was the answer everyone wanted. The test is declared a pass.

Let's clear up a myth right here. Myth: one person made one terrible mistake. Fact: investigators found a chain of failures. The cement. The skipped evaluation. The misread test. Each one alone might not have been enough. Together, they lined up like holes in slices of Swiss cheese.

8:02 p.m.

With the test "passed," the crew starts replacing the heavy drilling mud in the well with lighter seawater. That mud was the weight holding the oil and gas down. Now it's being taken away, while the cement at the bottom is failing.

Nine p.m. The VIPs head up to the bridge for a tour of its screens and technology.

And down below, the well is starting to talk. At 9:01, with the pumps steady, the pressure on the drill pipe starts to rise when it should be falling. It's the first sign of a kick: oil and gas forcing their way up.

Nobody catches it.

At 9:30, the driller notices something odd: a pressure difference between the drill pipe and the kill line. The crew shuts down the pumps to figure out why.

They have about ten minutes left.

[PAUSE 1.2 heartbeat]

Between 9:40 and 9:43, drilling mud comes shooting out of the floor, up the derrick, higher than the top of the tower.

[PAUSE 2.6 eruption]

The crew fights back. They close the preventers around the pipe. One of them finally seals. But gas is already above it, racing up the riser and pouring across the deck. A gas alarm sounds. Then another. And another.

At about 9:45, the assistant driller calls his boss. Mud is going to the crown, the top of the derrick.

At about 9:49 p.m., the gas finds a spark.

[PAUSE 4.0 explosion]

The power goes out. A second explosion. Then fire everywhere.

On the bridge, the alarm goes out over the speakers: this is not a drill.

[PAUSE 1.4 alarm]

There was still one last line of defense. On the seafloor, a mile below, sat the blowout preventer. A steel tower fifty-seven feet tall, weighing about four hundred tons. Its job, in an emergency, was to slice through the drill pipe and seal the well shut.

A crew member on the bridge hits the emergency disconnect button.

Nothing happens. The explosions had likely cut the lines to it.

An automatic backup system was supposed to take over. Investigators believe it did fire. But the drill pipe inside had buckled, bent off-center by the force of the flowing well. The blades couldn't cut through it cleanly. The last line of defense failed.

Now, the only thing left to do was to survive.

Men run to the lifeboats in the dark, through smoke and heat. The first lifeboat pulls away before everyone can reach it. Some men can't wait. They jump from the deck into the black water below.

A supply ship nearby, the Damon B. Bankston, turns toward the fire. Its crew pulls people out of the sea, and from the lifeboats and a life raft.

By 11:45 p.m., the lifeboats are empty. At the final headcount, eleven men are missing.

[PAUSE 1.5 silence]

They were never found.

One hundred and fifteen people survived. Many of them watched the rig burn for the rest of the night from the deck of that supply ship.

The Deepwater Horizon burned for about thirty-six hours. On April twenty-second, Earth Day, it rolled over and sank.

But the disaster was just beginning. Because a mile down, the well was still open.

For eighty-seven days, oil and gas poured into the Gulf of Mexico. Engineers tried domes, junk shots and top kills. Nothing worked, until a new cap finally stopped the flow on July fifteenth. By then, between three and five million barrels of oil had escaped. It became one of the worst environmental disasters in American history. Astronauts could see the slick from space.

So, why did it happen?

The investigators' answer is uncomfortable. The CSB, the government board that investigates chemical disasters, found that the companies measured safety mostly by counting injuries. Slips, trips and falls. And by that measure, the Deepwater Horizon was a model rig.

But the deadly risk wasn't a slip on a wet floor. It was a well full of oil and gas under pressure. And the investigators found that this kind of process safety did not get the same attention.

Seven years without a lost-time incident. Celebrated that very afternoon. And that very night, eleven men lost their lives.

Remember the email at 5:45 that morning? "We have completed the job, and it went well." The cement at the bottom of the well was the first barrier to fail. Everything that followed was a chance to catch it. And every chance was missed.

After the disaster, the United States rewrote its offshore drilling rules. Negative pressure tests, which weren't even required back then, became mandatory. Blowout preventers faced new standards. And the companies paid billions in fines and settlements.

But for eleven families, no rule will ever be enough.

Jason Anderson. Dale Burkeen. Donald Clark. Stephen Curtis. Gordon Jones. Roy Wyatt Kemp. Karl Kleppinger. Blair Manuel. Dewey Revette. Shane Roshto. Adam Weise.
