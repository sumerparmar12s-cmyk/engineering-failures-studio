# The Engine That Fell Off — UPS Flight 2976 (≈6 min)

## Narration (final)

November fourth, 2025. A cargo jet is racing down the runway in Louisville, Kentucky. Then, just as its nose lifts off the ground, the left engine tears completely off the wing. The jet barely clears the airport fence, and then it comes down.

But here is the most disturbing part. The part that broke had failed before. Not once. Again and again, for more than twenty years. So why was this plane still flying?

This is UPS Flight 2976.

The flight was bound for Honolulu, more than eight hours across the Pacific. The aircraft was a McDonnell Douglas MD-11, a three-engine widebody, with one engine under each wing and a third mounted high in the tail. This particular jet had been flying for thirty-four years: first carrying passengers for Thai Airways, then cargo for UPS. Almost ninety-three thousand hours in the air.

In the cockpit sat three experienced pilots, with more than thirty thousand flight hours between them. At about twelve minutes past five in the evening, they pushed the throttles forward.

Everything was normal. Until the moment the nose came up.

Airport cameras recorded what happened next. The entire left engine, and the pylon holding it, broke away from the wing. Burning, the engine flew up and over the top of the fuselage, and crashed down beside the runway. Fire erupted where the pylon had been attached.

In the cockpit, an engine fire warning. The crew pushed the two remaining engines harder. The jet was moving at more than two hundred miles an hour. But it would not climb. According to the flight data recorder, it never rose much more than thirty feet.

It cleared the blast fence at the end of the runway. Its landing gear tore through the roof of a warehouse. Then it slammed into a storage yard and a petroleum recycling facility, and burst into flames.

Nineteen seconds after the engine separated, the recording ended. The three pilots were killed, along with eleven people on the ground. A fifteenth victim died of injuries weeks later.

So what could tear an engine off a jet? Investigators found the answer in one small part.

Each wing engine hangs from a pylon. At the back, that pylon is held to the wing by a single joint: two metal lugs wrapped around a spherical bearing. A steel ball, inside a ring called the race.

On Flight 2976, that ring had split in two. Under the microscope, about three quarters of the fracture was fatigue: a crack that grew slowly, flight after flight, starting from a small groove machined inside the ring. The lugs around it showed fatigue cracks too. When they finally let go, nothing was left holding the back of the engine.

And this is where the story turns. Because this was not the first time that bearing had cracked.

Between 2002 and 2017, airlines reported fractures of the same bearing race on other MD-11s. In February 2011, Boeing sent operators a service letter describing four failures on three airplanes. Boeing had even designed a new bearing, without the groove.

But the letter reached a conclusion that would echo for years. A broken bearing, it said, would not result in a safety-of-flight condition. Airlines could still install the old design.

Then, according to evidence presented at the NTSB's hearing, Boeing asked the FAA in 2014 to stretch the inspection interval for those lugs, from nineteen thousand nine hundred flights to twenty-nine thousand two hundred. By then, five fractures had been reported. The FAA agreed.

The jet that crashed in Louisville had flown twenty-one thousand and forty-three flights. Under the old interval, its special inspection would already have been done. Under the new one, it was not yet due.

As one witness told the NTSB, there had been a misunderstanding, twenty years earlier, about how severe a failure of this bearing could be.

If this story sounds familiar, it should. In 1979, American Airlines Flight 191, a DC-10, the MD-11's direct ancestor, lost its left engine and pylon during takeoff in Chicago. Two hundred and seventy-three people died. The cause back then was different: damage from a maintenance shortcut. But the result was the same. An engine leaving the wing, at the worst possible moment.

Within days of the Louisville crash, the FAA grounded every MD-11 and DC-10 in the United States. In January 2026, UPS retired its entire MD-11 fleet. In May, with new inspection requirements in place, the FAA allowed the MD-11 to fly again.

The NTSB's final report, and its official probable cause, are still to come.

Nineteen seconds. Fifteen lives. And a warning written down fourteen years before the crash, that a broken bearing was nothing to worry about. Sometimes the most dangerous failures are the ones we have already decided are harmless.
