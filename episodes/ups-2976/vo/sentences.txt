 139.48 So what could tear an engine off a jet?
 141.98 Investigators found the answer in one small part.
 146.26 Each wing engine hangs from a pylon.
 149.06 At the back, that pylon is held to the wing by a single joint: two metal lugs wrapped around a spherical bearing.
 156.20 A steel ball, inside a ring called the race.
 160.04 On Flight 2976, that ring had split in two.
 164.82 Under the microscope, about three quarters of the fracture was fatigue: a crack that grew slowly, flight after flight, starting from a small groove machined inside the ring.
 175.48 The lugs around it showed fatigue cracks too.
 178.58 When they finally let go, nothing was left holding the back of the engine.
 183.24 And this is where the story turns.
 185.56 Because this was not the first time that bearing had cracked.
 189.82 Between 2002 and 2017, airlines reported fractures of the same bearing race on other MD-11s.
 198.16 In February 2011, Boeing sent operators a service letter describing four failures on three airplanes.
 207.08 Boeing had even designed a new bearing, without the groove.
 210.86 But the letter reached a conclusion that would echo for years.
 214.52 A broken bearing, it said, would not result in a safety-of-flight condition.
 219.98 Airlines could still install the old design.
 223.22 Then, according to evidence presented at the NTSB's hearing, Boeing asked the FAA in 2014 to stretch the inspection interval for those lugs, from nineteen thousand nine hundred flights to twenty-nine thousand two hundred.
 238.50 By then, five fractures had been reported.
 241.84 The FAA agreed.
 243.84 The jet that crashed in Louisville had flown twenty-one thousand and forty-three flights.
 249.28 Under the old interval, its special inspection would already have been done.
